# Upstream positioning for strategic UX and service design

## What we are actually selling

We are not selling UX as interface polish at the end of a project. We are selling an upstream discovery phase that helps a client decide what to build, why it matters, and what must change across the service for the solution to work.

That makes the offer relevant before delivery starts, when the biggest risks are still cheap to address.

## The risk of moving too quickly from signals to delivery

Many organizations consider redesign or development because they can see real signs that something is not working well enough:

* Adoption is lower than expected
* Support effort is rising
* Customers experience the service as inconsistent across channels
* Teams are pulling in different directions on priorities and scope
* A legacy workflow is creating delay, cost, or operational friction

Those are valid reasons to act. The mistake is not noticing them. The mistake is treating them as enough evidence for a predefined solution. If delivery starts too early, the client often funds build work before they have confidence in the problem definition, the target journey, or the operational consequences of the change.

## Who typically buys this

* **Managing director or CEO:** Needs more confidence that investment is going into the right problem
* **Product, digital, or transformation lead:** Needs a better basis for prioritization, scope, and sequencing
* **Commercial or service lead:** Needs clearer evidence of where the experience is helping or hurting conversion, retention, or service load
* **Operations lead:** Needs visibility into the process and ownership issues behind the customer experience
* **Technology lead:** Needs earlier clarity on dependencies, data needs, and delivery risk

## When to position the offer

The offer is most relevant when:

* A redesign is being discussed, but the problem is still loosely defined
* Several departments influence the experience and no one has the full picture
* The client wants to improve a journey, but the real issue may sit in process, policy, content, or handoffs
* Teams are jumping to features without a shared view of user need or business priority
* A major delivery investment needs a stronger strategic basis

## Why design thinking and service design matter

Design thinking helps frame the right problem, surface assumptions, and test solution directions before the organization commits too much time and money.

Service design makes the broader system visible. It looks at the full journey and the supporting operations behind it, so the client does not mistake a service problem for a screen problem.

Together, these disciplines help clients make better decisions because they connect user value, business value, and operational reality in one process.

## What the client should experience

The client should experience discovery as a managed decision process rather than as a bundle of disconnected workshops.

That means:

* A clear decision frame and agreed scope at the start
* A focused sequence of activities with the right people in the room
* Structured synthesis between activities so input is turned into decision-ready material
* Review points where the client confirms what is true, what is still uncertain, and what the next step should be

This is important because discovery only creates business value when each activity produces a concrete output that supports the next decision.

## The promise we can make

We can credibly promise:

* Better decisions before delivery begins
* Clearer alignment across business, delivery, and operational stakeholders
* Reduced waste from avoidable rework and late changes
* A more grounded basis for prioritization and investment
* Tangible concept material that helps the organization move forward with confidence

## The promise we should not make

We should not present discovery as a guarantee of certainty. It is a way to reduce risk, improve clarity, and increase the odds of making the right investment decision.

We should also avoid presenting discovery as research for its own sake. The value lies in the decisions it enables, the risks it reduces, and the path it creates into delivery.
