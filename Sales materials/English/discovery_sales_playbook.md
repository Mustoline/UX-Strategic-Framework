# Discovery sales playbook for strategic UX and service design

## How to open the conversation

Open from the client's business problem, not from UX capability.

Useful entry point:

"Before we discuss features or redesign scope, we help clients clarify where the real problem sits, which users and journeys matter most, and what needs to change in the service for delivery to be worth the investment."

## Qualification questions

Use these questions to understand whether the client has a real discovery need:

* What business decision needs to be made in the next 4-8 weeks?
* What signals tell you the current experience is not working as it should?
* Which users, customers, or internal teams are most affected?
* Where do stakeholders currently disagree on priorities or direction?
* What evidence already exists, and what is still based on assumption?
* Which constraints are already fixed, and which still need to be confirmed?
* Who can review and confirm outputs quickly enough between steps to keep the work moving?
* How many departments, systems, or handoffs shape the experience today?
* What budget or delivery initiative depends on getting this right?
* What would success look like 6-12 months after implementation?

## How to recommend a package

* **Recommend Package A** when the scope is narrow, time is short, and the main need is a focused decision
* **Recommend Package B** when the client needs to define the right concept for a product, service, or journey before delivery scope can be set
* **Recommend Package C** when the challenge spans channels, departments, workflows, or operating model decisions

## How to explain how the work runs

In commercial conversations, explain the work as a managed sequence rather than as a list of workshops.

Useful language:

"This is not a workshop bundle. We run a small number of focused activities, synthesize what comes out of each one, and bring back concrete outputs for review before the next step starts."

Keep the explanation focused on:

* A clear decision to support
* The few activities needed to generate the right input
* Concrete outputs after each step
* Fast review and validation so the work keeps moving

Do not explain the internal production mechanics behind the scenes. The client only needs to understand the delivery logic, the outputs, and their role in keeping the work moving.

## Common objections and grounded responses

### "We already know what users need"

That may be true, but the real question is how certain the client is and what that confidence is based on. In many organizations, what feels like user knowledge is actually a mix of internal assumptions, stakeholder opinions, support input, and second-hand signals. Discovery helps test whether that picture reflects real user needs, behaviors, and barriers before the client commits significant delivery budget.

### "We do not have time for discovery"

If the client is about to commit meaningful delivery budget, the real question is whether they can afford not to spend a short period reducing uncertainty first. Discovery is usually measured in days or weeks. The cost of moving ahead on unclear assumptions is often much higher: rework, slower decisions, extra stakeholder rounds, and scope changes once delivery is underway.

### "We need screens, not strategy"

Screens can absolutely be part of the deliverable, but they only help if they address the right problem. If the real issue sits in process, content, policy, ownership, or handoffs, then producing screens first risks making the wrong thing look more polished. Discovery helps ensure any interface work is tied to a real user and service problem worth solving.

### "Can we skip research?"

Sometimes yes, if the decision is small and there is already recent, relevant evidence the client trusts. That is often when Package A is enough. But on a larger or riskier decision, skipping research usually means moving ahead without testing whether internal assumptions match real user conditions. That may still be the choice, but it should be a conscious trade-off rather than an unexamined default.

## What should always be in the proposal

Every proposal should make these points clear:

* The business problem and why it matters now
* The decision the discovery work is meant to support
* The users, journeys, or service areas in scope
* The methods we will use and why they fit the problem
* How the work will run as focused activities, synthesis between those activities, and review and validation points
* The concrete outcomes the client will receive
* The level of client involvement required
* How the work will support prioritization, roadmap decisions, and delivery planning

## Discovery-to-delivery handoff

A good discovery phase should leave the client with more than insight. It should produce practical decision support for the next phase:

* Problem frame and success criteria
* Journey or service maps that explain the current state
* Target-state concept material
* Prioritized opportunities and recommended sequencing
* Key risks, assumptions, and dependencies
* Clear implications for delivery scope and ownership

## Measures of success

The strongest proof that discovery created value is visible after the work is complete. Useful measures include:

* Fewer major scope changes once delivery starts
* Faster internal decisions on priorities
* Better alignment across business, operations, and delivery teams
* Lower support load or internal effort in the targeted journey
* Stronger adoption, conversion, or task completion after implementation

## Proposal language

Use language like this in proposals and conversations:

"This discovery phase is designed to reduce the risk of moving too quickly into delivery without enough clarity. We will define the problem, understand the most important user and service dynamics, test the future-state direction, and give you a practical basis for prioritization and next-step investment. The work will run through focused activities, structured synthesis between those activities, and a concrete review point after each step so decisions are made on confirmed findings rather than assumptions."
