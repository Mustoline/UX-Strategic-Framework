# Strategic UX and service design discovery packages

## Why discovery belongs upstream

We position UX upstream by selling discovery as a decision phase, not as a design add-on. Before a client invests in delivery, we help them understand the business problem, the user need, and the service implications behind the interface. The point is to reduce the risk of funding the wrong solution, shorten internal decision cycles, and create a clearer basis for prioritization.

A strong discovery phase should answer:

* What problem are we solving, and why does it matter now?
* Which users, customers, or internal teams are most affected?
* Where in the current journey is value won or lost?
* What must change in process, content, data, or operations for the solution to work?
* What should be built now, later, or not at all?

## What clients are buying

Clients are not buying more UX activity for its own sake. They are buying:

* A clearer problem definition before budget is committed
* Better evidence on user needs, pain points, and decision moments
* Visibility into service and operational dependencies early in the process
* A tested target-state concept that reduces rework in delivery
* A practical recommendation for what to prioritize next

## How the packages are delivered

Each package should be run as a staged sequence of dependent activities rather than as a loose list of tasks.

The client-facing delivery model has three visible parts:

* A clear package frame stating the decision to support, the scope, the activities, and the expected outcomes
* A focused sequence of workshops, interviews, evidence reviews, or working sessions that generate the input needed for the next step
* Structured synthesis between activities, followed by a review checkpoint before the next activity starts

In practice, that means:

* The client provides the input needed for the next activity through workshops, interviews, internal evidence, or stakeholder review
* We turn that input into a concrete output such as a decision frame, evidence baseline, journey map, concept, roadmap, or recommendation
* The client reviews and validates that output before the next activity starts
* Unknown constraints are recorded as risks to resolve early rather than treated as proof that no constraints exist
* If required input is missing or the output is not yet agreed, the next activity pauses so the work does not move forward on unconfirmed assumptions

This keeps the package commercially grounded and makes the sequence explicit: each step depends on confirmed output from the previous one.

## Package portfolio

### Package A: Discovery sprint

* **Best for:** One clearly bounded problem in a single feature area, workflow, or journey step where the team needs a go or no-go decision and recommended scope before development starts
* **Core activities:**
    * Run a 90-minute sponsor workshop with 3-5 decision-makers to define the business question, target users, success criteria, and constraints
    * Review existing evidence from analytics, support issues, sales input, and prior research relevant to the selected problem
    * Run a 60-90 minute mapping session of the selected journey step to show where users or staff lose time, confidence, or momentum
    * Run a 2-hour option review to compare 1-2 solution directions against agreed business, user, and delivery criteria
    * Prepare a short recommendation document with a simple concept view or clickable prototype showing what to build next, what to defer, and what still needs validation
* **Timeline:** 5 business days
* **Concrete outcomes:**
    * A 1-2 page recommendation document stating the decision, recommended scope, target users, success criteria, and what not to build yet
    * A one-page map of the selected journey step showing the 3-5 biggest breakdowns causing delay, confusion, or internal effort
    * A simple concept view or 1-2 screen clickable prototype illustrating the recommended improvement
    * A short risk and next-step list covering assumptions, dependencies, and checks to complete before implementation
* **Client involvement:** Approximately 4-6 hours
* **Working model:** Each activity depends on validated output from the previous step, with a client review checkpoint before the work moves on

### Package B: Service concept definition

* **Best for:** A product, portal, or service journey where the opportunity is known, but the user need, future-state flow, and supporting service changes are not yet clear enough to scope delivery
* **Core activities:**
    * Run a 2-hour scoping workshop with the sponsor and key stakeholders to agree the journey in scope, business case, and success criteria
    * Conduct 4-6 semi-structured user interviews, typically 45-60 minutes each, run remotely or in person, supported by a review of analytics, customer feedback, support issues, and sales input
    * Map the current journey to show where the experience and service break down across users, teams, and systems
    * Run a 2-3 hour concept working session to define the future-state journey and the required changes in process, content, ownership, and data
    * Build a clickable prototype of the core flow and run a 2-hour prioritization workshop to decide what should be delivered first
* **Timeline:** 2 weeks
* **Concrete outcomes:**
    * A concise insight summary from 4-6 interviews showing the top user needs, barriers, and decision criteria
    * A current-state and future-state journey map showing how the experience should change end to end
    * A list of required service changes across process, content, ownership, and data, with the main dependencies called out
    * A clickable prototype of the core flow illustrating the proposed experience at the key decision points
    * A prioritized delivery recommendation showing what to build first, what to defer, and what to validate next
* **Client involvement:** Approximately 1.5-2 days
* **Working model:** Each activity depends on validated output from the previous step, with a client review checkpoint before the work moves on

### Package C: Strategic service redesign

* **Best for:** A service redesign or transformation effort that crosses departments, channels, or systems and needs a basis for investment, sequencing, and organizational change
* **Core activities:**
    * Conduct 5-7 executive and service-owner interviews, typically 45-60 minutes each, to clarify the business case, strategic priorities, and investment questions
    * Run a half-day ecosystem workshop to agree the service scope across departments, touchpoints, systems, and operational actors
    * Conduct 5-8 contextual interviews, observations, or shadowing sessions, typically 60-90 minutes each, supported by a review of analytics, support patterns, and operational data
    * Build a current-state service blueprint to expose delays, duplication, cost, and ownership gaps
    * Run a half-day future-state service model workshop and validate the concept with 5-8 users, customers, or internal stakeholders
    * Run a half-day roadmap and business case session to create a phased roadmap, outline change implications, and frame the ROI hypothesis
* **Timeline:** Up to 4 weeks
* **Concrete outcomes:**
    * An executive brief summarizing the service problem, the value at stake, and the main investment questions
    * A current-state service blueprint showing where delays, duplication, costs, and ownership gaps damage the experience
    * A tested future-state service model supported by concept feedback from 5-8 users, customers, or internal stakeholders
    * A phased roadmap across teams and systems showing sequencing, dependencies, and major decision points
    * A change and business case summary covering operating model implications, ownership changes, and the ROI hypothesis
* **Client involvement:** Approximately 3-4 days
* **Working model:** Each activity depends on validated output from the previous step, with a client review checkpoint before the work moves on

## How the packages build on design thinking and service design

Our approach combines two disciplines:

* **Design thinking:** Clarifies the right problem, makes assumptions visible, and tests solution directions before teams overcommit
* **Service design:** Looks beyond the interface and exposes the people, processes, data, and handoffs required to make the experience work in practice

Together, they help clients make better delivery decisions because the concept is shaped by user reality, business priorities, and operational constraints at the same time.

## How to choose the right package

* Choose **Package A** when one part of the experience needs a fast but grounded decision before development begins
* Choose **Package B** when the whole journey or product concept needs to be defined before scope and priorities can be set
* Choose **Package C** when the initiative requires changes across the service model, not only the interface
