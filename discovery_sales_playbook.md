# Discovery sales playbook for strategic UX and service design

## How to open the conversation

Open from the client's business problem, not from UX capability.

Useful entry point:

"Before we discuss features or redesign scope, we help clients clarify where the real problem sits, which users and journeys matter most, and what needs to change in the service for delivery to be worth the investment."

## Qualification questions

Use these questions to understand whether the client has a real discovery need:

* What business decision needs to be made in the next 4-8 weeks?
* What signals tell you the current experience is not working as it should?
* Which users, customers, or internal teams are most affected?
* Where do stakeholders currently disagree on priorities or direction?
* What evidence already exists, and what is still based on assumption?
* How many departments, systems, or handoffs shape the experience today?
* What budget or delivery initiative depends on getting this right?
* What would success look like 6-12 months after implementation?

## How to recommend a package

* **Recommend Package A** when the scope is narrow, time is short, and the main need is a focused decision
* **Recommend Package B** when the client needs to define the right concept for a product, service, or journey before delivery scope can be set
* **Recommend Package C** when the challenge spans channels, departments, workflows, or operating model decisions

## Common objections and grounded responses

### "We already know what users need"

That may be true in parts of the journey, but discovery is often what reveals where internal assumptions differ and where operational constraints will undermine a good concept. The question is not whether the team knows something. The question is whether they know enough to invest with confidence.

### "We do not have time for discovery"

If the client is about to invest heavily in delivery, then they do have time for discovery. A short discovery phase usually costs less than late scope changes, internal misalignment, or building the wrong thing first.

### "We need screens, not strategy"

Screens can be part of the deliverable, but screens alone rarely solve the problem. If the issue sits in process, content, policy, ownership, or handoffs, then interface work without discovery simply hides the real issue for a while.

### "Can we skip research?"

Sometimes yes, if the problem is small and strong evidence already exists. That is exactly when Package A is useful. But skipping research on a larger decision usually means the organization is choosing speed over confidence without being explicit about the trade-off.

## What should always be in the proposal

Every proposal should make these points clear:

* The business problem and why it matters now
* The decision the discovery work is meant to support
* The users, journeys, or service areas in scope
* The methods we will use and why they fit the problem
* The concrete outcomes the client will receive
* The level of client involvement required
* How the work will support prioritization, roadmap decisions, and delivery planning

## Discovery-to-delivery handoff

A good discovery phase should leave the client with more than insight. It should produce practical decision support for the next phase:

* Problem frame and success criteria
* Journey or service maps that explain the current state
* Target-state concept material
* Prioritized opportunities and recommended sequencing
* Key risks, assumptions, and dependencies
* Clear implications for delivery scope and ownership

## Measures of success

The strongest proof that discovery created value is visible after the work is complete. Useful measures include:

* Fewer major scope changes once delivery starts
* Faster internal decisions on priorities
* Better alignment across business, operations, and delivery teams
* Lower support load or internal effort in the targeted journey
* Stronger adoption, conversion, or task completion after implementation

## Proposal language

Use language like this in proposals and conversations:

"This discovery phase is designed to reduce the risk of moving too quickly into delivery without enough clarity. We will define the problem, understand the most important user and service dynamics, test the future-state direction, and give you a practical basis for prioritization and next-step investment."
